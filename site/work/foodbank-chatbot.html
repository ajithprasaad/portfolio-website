Service Automation.

A RAG-based chatbot that answers the Capital Area Food Bank's repetitive support questions, built from historical JIRA tickets with intelligent triage and human handoff.

00 / context

The Capital Area Food Bank is the largest hunger-relief organization in the Washington, DC metro area. In 2020, COVID-19 upended operations overnight: fresh produce distribution surged from 2 million to 6.4 million pounds, and total donations exceeded 32 million pounds, roughly 78 million meals across 12 warehouses. The organization needed to scale direct service while running with 40% fewer volunteers.

01Problem

Front-line staff were spending hours each day answering the same questions: order status, volunteer scheduling windows, donation drop-off procedures.

Forty percent of tickets required escalation to already-at-capacity specialists. Information was scattered across JIRA, email, and tribal knowledge. With no self-serve option, a volunteer with a simple scheduling question waited in the same queue as a complex logistics issue.

03Approach
01

RAG over fine-tuning

The knowledge base evolves constantly, and answers must stay grounded in verifiable past resolutions, not creatively generated.

02

Multi-provider LLMs

DeepSeek as primary, with OpenAI and Gemini as fallbacks. This gives cost flexibility and resilience without sacrificing quality.

03

Intelligent triage

A triage layer decides whether the bot can answer confidently or a human should take over.

04Evaluation & guardrails

Confidence scoring

High confidence (≥ 0.65) auto-resolves. Medium (0.45–0.65) passes through sentiment & urgency analysis. Low (< 0.45) escalates immediately.

Feedback loop

Thumbs up/down collected on every response and persisted for continuous improvement. Provider switching cuts token costs.

07Reflections

A curated golden-set evaluation pipeline would accelerate confidence calibration. The feedback loop works, but systematic test sets compress the learning cycle. It's a change I'd make before any production rollout.

The multi-provider pattern is broadly applicable to any nonprofit with an evolving knowledge base and a limited budget. The same architecture could serve legal-aid clinics, community health lines, or university advising.
